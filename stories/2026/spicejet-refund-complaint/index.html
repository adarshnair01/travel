How I got my full ₹15,000 back after SpiceJet cancelled my flight (and Goibibo refunded ₹2,526)

SpiceJet cancelled our Delhi–Bengaluru flight and Goibibo offered back ₹2,526 of the ₹15,000 we paid. Here's exactly how I escalated it, what finally worked, the passenger rules that apply, and templates you can copy for your own SpiceJet refund complaint.

This one isn’t a trip story. It’s about a trip that didn’t happen, and the two weeks I spent trying to get my own money back.

SpiceJet cancelled our flight from Delhi to Bengaluru. We had booked it through Goibibo for two people and paid ₹15,000. When the refund showed up on Goibibo, it was ₹2,526. The other ₹12,474 had simply been “deducted”, for a flight that we didn’t cancel and that never flew.

I eventually got every rupee back. It came in three parts, after a week of posting on X every hour or two, being stonewalled by an AI phone bot, and finally tagging the Ministry of Civil Aviation. I’m writing it all down because thousands of people are in the same position right now, and because the thing that finally worked wasn’t obvious when I started.

The short version: Passengers should not have to fight an airline, a booking platform and an AI chatbot just to receive a refund for a flight that the airline cancelled. But if you’re stuck in that fight, here’s what worked for me, step by step.

What happened: the booking and the cancellation#

Here are the basics (I’ve left out the PNR, booking ID and passenger details on purpose, and you should too when you post about yours):
Airline SpiceJet
Flight SG 142, New Delhi (DEL) to Bengaluru (BLR)
Travel date 5 November 2026
Passengers 2
Booked through Goibibo
Total paid ₹15,000
Cancelled by SpiceJet (the airline)

The flight was cancelled by the airline, and Goibibo’s own booking page said so. But the refund breakdown on the same page showed this:

Refund as Goibibo first showed it Amount
Amount paid ₹15,000
“Deductions” ₹12,474
Refund ₹2,526

That made no sense. Under India’s rules, when an airline cancels a flight, the passenger is entitled to a full refund or an alternative flight (more on the rules below). So I went to SpiceJet’s refund portal to see what they had done. It said:

  • PNR status: CLOSED
  • Refund status: REFUNDED
  • Refund amount: blank

So the airline said the money had been refunded, the booking platform said most of it was gone, and nobody could tell me where ₹12,474 had gone. That gap, between “the airline has refunded” and “the money is in your account”, is where a lot of passengers get stuck.

The biggest problem: I couldn’t reach a human at Goibibo#

I want to spend some time on this, because it was the most frustrating part of the whole thing, and I think it’s the part that matters most for other passengers.

I had a simple, specific, financial question: the airline cancelled my flight, so why are you refunding ₹2,526 of ₹15,000? I needed a person who could look at the booking and fix it. I couldn’t find one.

  • No visible human support channel. I couldn’t find a way in the app or on the website to reach a person who could investigate a refund dispute.
  • No support email for escalation. I looked for an email address to send a written complaint with screenshots. I couldn’t find one.
  • An AI bot on the phone. When I called customer support, an AI voice bot answered. When it didn’t understand my request, which was most of the time, it went around in circles, asking me the same questions again.
  • No way to register a formal complaint. The bot never gave me a complaint number, a clear way to escalate, or a way to reach someone who could actually look at the deduction.

AI chatbots are fine for routine things: “Where’s my ticket?”, “What’s my baggage allowance?”. They should never become a wall between a customer and the company, especially when the question is about money the company is holding.

If you are a travel agent, customers expect answers from you, not an endless conversation with a chatbot. You took the booking. You took the payment. When the flight is cancelled, the passenger has no one else to turn to. That is the moment your service actually matters.

Here’s my concern, and I want to be clear that it’s my concern, not a proven fact. When support is this hard to reach, fewer people will challenge a deduction. Many will give up and accept ₹2,526, because chasing the rest costs more time and stress than they can spare. I don’t know whether that’s the intention. But that is the effect, and it lands hardest on the people who can least afford it.

What I tried first: posting on X (and why it didn’t work at first)#

With no other channel, I went to X (Twitter). I started posting on 17 November and kept going, every hour or two, tagging @flyspicejet and @goibibo. I replied to their posts, explained the refund gap, and shared the numbers.

My first post said what many passengers were saying: that a huge number of people had been hit by SpiceJet’s cancellations and were chasing refunds, and that airline-cancelled flights should be refunded in full, with zero deductions. It got more attention than I expected, over 8,700 views and dozens of replies from other passengers. One reply pointed out that some passengers were also losing convenience fees, insurance and paid-seat charges in their refunds.

(In that post I used a figure of about 50,000 affected passengers, based on what people were saying at the time. I haven’t been able to confirm that number from an official source, so please treat it as a passenger estimate, not a fact.)

But for my own refund, nothing moved. Goibibo didn’t give me a meaningful answer. Days went by.

What finally worked: tagging the Ministry of Civil Aviation#

On 19 November I changed my approach. Instead of tagging only the airline and the booking platform, I tagged all three:

  • @flyspicejet (the airline)
  • @goibibo (the booking platform)
  • @MoCA_GoI (the Ministry of Civil Aviation, Government of India)

My post said, plainly, that the flight was cancelled by the airline, that I was still chasing my own money, and that I wanted the full ₹15,000 back with no unjustified deductions.

Two replies came the same day:

  • The Ministry of Civil Aviation replied, apologised for the inconvenience, and asked me to send my PNR, name and phone number by direct message so the issue could be looked into.
  • SpiceJet replied that it had already processed the full refund of ₹16,900 to the travel agency’s account (MakeMyTrip, Goibibo’s parent company) on 16 November, and that I should take up my refund with the travel agency.

That second reply changed everything. It meant the airline wasn’t holding my money: it had refunded the full fare to the agency three days earlier. (Why SpiceJet’s figure was ₹16,900 when I paid ₹15,000, I don’t know. It may be because the ticket price on the airline’s side differed from what I paid after offers on Goibibo. Either way, it was more than I paid.) The question was now very simple: why was Goibibo passing on only ₹2,526?

I can’t say what happened inside the Ministry, SpiceJet or Goibibo after that, and I’m not claiming the Ministry personally intervened in my case. But tagging the Ministry was the point at which I got a clear, written, public answer about where my money was. I believe it helped bring my complaint to the right people.

How the money came back: three parts#

With SpiceJet’s public confirmation in hand, I went back to Goibibo on X, quoted the airline’s reply, and asked them to process the outstanding amount. When they asked, I sent the booking details and screenshots by DM. Then:

Part When Amount
1. Goibibo’s default refund After the cancellation about ₹2,500 (shown as ₹2,526)
2. After the Ministry post and SpiceJet’s reply Within a day about ₹11,500
3. After I replied to a Goibibo executive’s email The next day the remaining ~₹1,000
Total ₹15,000

The last ₹1,000 needed one more nudge. After the Ministry post, a Goibibo executive emailed me about the refund, and his own email address was in the signature at the end. That was the first direct email contact I’d had with anyone at Goibibo. I replied to him, pointing out that the total was still short of what I’d paid and asking for the remaining amount. It arrived the next day.

Tip: If anyone from the platform emails you, reply to that thread and save the address. A named person’s email is worth far more than a support bot.

From the day I tagged the Ministry to the last rupee: about two days. Before that, more than a week of getting nowhere.

Takeaway: “The airline has refunded” and “the money is in your account” are two different things. If you booked through an agent or app, find out where your refund actually is: with the airline, or with the agent.

This was my individual experience. It isn’t a guaranteed process, and tagging the Ministry won’t get everyone a refund. But it’s a sequence of steps anyone can follow, and the formal channels below work whether or not anyone replies on X.

Your rights: what the rules say about refunds for cancelled flights#

India’s passenger rights are set by the Directorate General of Civil Aviation (DGCA) in documents called Civil Aviation Requirements (CARs). Two matter here. I’m not a lawyer, and you should read the current text on the DGCA website before relying on any of this, but here’s what they say in plain language.

Refunds when you booked through an agent or app#

The DGCA’s refund rules (CAR Section 3, Series M, Part II) were revised in February 2026. As reported when they came out, they say:

  • When you book through a travel agent or portal, the airline is responsible for the refund, because agents are its appointed representatives, and the airline must make sure the refund is completed within 14 working days.
  • For tickets bought directly with a credit card, the airline must refund within 7 days.
  • Keeping your money as a credit shell is your choice, not the airline’s default.
  • Cancellation charges can never be more than the basic fare plus fuel surcharge. Agent charges are separate, but they must be fully disclosed at the time of booking.
  • Statutory taxes and airport fees (UDF, ADF, PSF) must be refunded on cancellation, even on non-refundable fares.

When the airline cancels the flight#

The rules on cancellations and delays (CAR Section 3, Series M, Part IV) say, in short:

  • When the airline cancels, it must offer you an alternative flight or a full refund. Cancellation charges are for when you cancel, not the airline.
  • If you’re told about the cancellation at short notice (less than 24 hours before departure, or without the offer of a suitable alternative flight), you may also be entitled to compensation of ₹5,000, ₹7,500 or ₹10,000 per passenger, depending on the flight’s block time (capped at the one-way basic fare plus fuel charge).
  • Compensation doesn’t apply when the cancellation is caused by extraordinary circumstances beyond the airline’s control, such as bad weather or security issues.

What this means, and what it doesn’t#

  • Statutory rights are what the airline must give you under the DGCA rules: a full refund or alternative flight when it cancels, within set timelines.
  • Booking-platform terms are the agent’s own rules: convenience fees, insurance add-ons and service charges. Some of these may legitimately be non-refundable if they were disclosed when you booked. Not every deduction is automatically illegal.
  • My experience is just that: what happened in my case.

In my case, the airline confirmed in writing that it had refunded the full fare to the agency. At that point there was no fare-related reason for a deduction of ₹12,474, and the agency eventually refunded it all.

Why this matters beyond my ₹15,000#

SpiceJet’s problems this month have been serious. On 17 November, the DGCA issued SpiceJet a show-cause notice over repeated cancellations and delays, poor passenger support during disruptions, and a largely grounded fleet. Reports said its on-time performance on 19 November was just 25%.

Behind every cancelled flight are people who can’t just shrug it off:

  • Families heading to weddings, funerals and festivals, with elderly parents or small children.
  • Business travellers who miss meetings they can’t reschedule.
  • Students travelling for exams, interviews and admissions, where missing the date can cost far more than the ticket.
  • People forced to buy last-minute replacement tickets at two or three times the normal fare, paying twice for the same journey while their first payment is stuck.
  • Passengers who lose hotel bookings, trains and connecting flights that were planned around the cancelled one.
  • Anyone for whom ₹10,000–15,000 is a lot of money, which is most people. Having it locked up in a refund dispute for weeks is a real hardship.

None of this is fixed by a chatbot. It needs three things: support that a human can actually reach, refund breakdowns that show exactly what is being deducted and why, and accountability when the money doesn’t arrive.

Step-by-step: how to chase your own SpiceJet refund#

Here’s the process I’d follow now, knowing what I know.

  1. Collect your evidence first. Booking confirmation, payment receipt or bank statement, the airline’s cancellation SMS or email, and screenshots of the refund breakdown on the booking app. See the checklist below.
  2. Check the airline’s refund portal. Enter your PNR on SpiceJet’s refund status page and screenshot what it shows: PNR status, refund status and amount.
  3. Compare the two. If the airline says “refunded” but the booking app shows a much smaller refund, write down both figures and the difference. That difference is your complaint.
  4. Post one clear complaint on X. Keep it short and factual: what happened, what you paid, what you’ve been offered, what you want.
  5. Tag all three: @flyspicejet, @goibibo (or whichever platform you used) and @MoCA_GoI.
  6. Put the numbers in the post. Amount paid, refund offered, amount deducted. Numbers are harder to ignore than anger.
  7. Attach screenshots, but hide personal information. Blur or crop the PNR, booking ID, names, phone number and email before you post.
  8. Follow up publicly, but don’t spam. Reply to your own post or to their replies with new information (“SpiceJet confirms the full refund went to the agency on 16 November”) rather than posting the same text again and again.
  9. Share booking details privately, only through verified accounts. When the official account (check for the verification badge) asks you to DM, send the PNR and booking ID there. Never send card details or OTPs, and beware of fake “support” accounts that reply to you.
  10. Raise a formal grievance on AirSewa. It’s the Ministry of Civil Aviation’s official passenger grievance portal, also available as an app. You get a complaint number and the complaint goes to the airline.
  11. Keep every reference number and written reply. Complaint IDs, DM replies, emails and the airline’s confirmation of the refund date and amount.
  12. Escalate to the National Consumer Helpline if it’s still unresolved. You can file online, on the NCH app, or call 1915. If that fails too, you can go to the consumer commission through e-Daakhil.
  13. Check that the full amount actually reached your original payment method. Don’t stop at “refund processed”. Check your bank or card statement and compare the total with what you paid.

Airline refunded vs. you refunded: When you book through an agent, the airline usually sends the refund to the agent, and the agent pays you. So “REFUNDED” on the airline’s portal may only mean the money has reached the agent. Ask the airline for the date and amount it refunded and who it went to, then hold the agent to that figure.

Templates you can copy#

Edit the details in brackets. Keep the tone firm and factual: it works better than shouting.

First complaint on X:

@flyspicejet cancelled my flight [SG XXX, CITY–CITY, DATE]. I paid ₹[AMOUNT] on @goibibo, but the refund shown is only ₹[REFUND], a deduction of ₹[DEDUCTION] on an AIRLINE-cancelled flight.

Under DGCA rules, airline cancellations mean a full refund. Please refund ₹[AMOUNT] in full.

@MoCA_GoI

Follow-up post:

Day [X] and still no full refund. @flyspicejet says the refund for my cancelled flight was processed to the agency on [DATE]. @goibibo, why has only ₹[REFUND] of ₹[AMOUNT] reached me?

Please credit the remaining ₹[BALANCE]. @MoCA_GoI

DM to the airline or booking platform:

Hello, my flight [SG XXX, CITY–CITY, DATE] was cancelled by the airline.

PNR: [PNR]
Booking ID: [BOOKING ID]
Passengers: [NUMBER]
Amount paid: ₹[AMOUNT]
Refund received so far: ₹[REFUND]
Outstanding: ₹[BALANCE]

Screenshots of the booking, payment and refund breakdown are attached. Please process the full refund and confirm the date and amount in writing.

Formal email escalation:

Subject: Full refund due for airline-cancelled flight – PNR [PNR], Booking ID [BOOKING ID]

Dear [Grievance Officer / Customer Support],

My flight [SG XXX] from [CITY] to [CITY] on [DATE], booked through [PLATFORM] for [NUMBER] passengers, was cancelled by [AIRLINE]. I paid ₹[AMOUNT].

The refund credited/shown is ₹[REFUND], leaving ₹[BALANCE] unpaid. [AIRLINE] has confirmed that it processed the full refund to [AGENCY] on [DATE] (screenshot attached).

As the cancellation was made by the airline, I request a full refund of the outstanding ₹[BALANCE] to my original payment method within 7 days, with a written breakdown of any deduction you believe applies and the basis for it.

If this is not resolved, I will escalate the matter through AirSewa and the National Consumer Helpline.

Attached: booking confirmation, payment receipt, cancellation notice, refund breakdown, airline's refund confirmation.

Regards,
[NAME]
[PHONE]

Evidence checklist#

  • Booking confirmation (email or PDF) showing the fare and passengers
  • Payment proof: card or UPI receipt, or bank statement
  • The airline’s cancellation SMS or email, with date and time
  • Screenshot of the booking app showing “cancelled by airline”
  • Screenshot of the refund breakdown, with the deductions visible
  • Screenshot of the airline’s refund status page
  • The airline’s written confirmation of when and to whom it refunded
  • Links to your X posts and screenshots of every reply
  • AirSewa and NCH complaint numbers
  • Receipts for any replacement tickets, hotels or transport you lost

Frequently asked questions#

Is SpiceJet required to give a full refund if it cancels my flight?#

Under the DGCA’s rules, when an airline cancels a flight, it must offer you an alternative flight or a full refund. Cancellation charges apply when you cancel, not when the airline does. Some booking-platform fees may be treated separately if they were disclosed when you booked.

How long does a SpiceJet refund take?#

Under the DGCA refund rules revised in 2026, refunds for tickets bought through an agent or portal should be completed within 14 working days, and credit-card refunds for direct bookings within 7 days. In practice, check the airline’s refund status page and chase both the airline and the agent if it takes longer.

SpiceJet’s portal says “REFUNDED” but I haven’t received anything. Why?#

If you booked through an app like Goibibo, MakeMyTrip or Cleartrip, the airline usually refunds the agent, not you. Ask the airline (publicly or by DM) for the refund date, amount and who it went to, then take that confirmation to the agent.

Goibibo refund not received: what can I do?#

Post a clear complaint on X tagging @goibibo, the airline and @MoCA_GoI, share your booking details by DM with the verified account when asked, and file on AirSewa and the National Consumer Helpline. Keep all the reference numbers.

How do I file an AirSewa complaint?#

Go to airsewa.gov.in or download the AirSewa app, register, choose “grievance”, select the airline, and describe the issue with your PNR, flight details and screenshots. You’ll get a complaint number to track it.

Can I complain to the Ministry of Civil Aviation on X?#

Yes. @MoCA_GoI responds to passenger complaints on X, often asking for your PNR and details by DM. It’s not a guarantee of a refund, but in my case it was the point at which I got a clear, public answer from the airline.

Am I entitled to compensation as well as a refund?#

Possibly, if the airline cancelled at short notice (under 24 hours, or without offering a suitable alternative flight) and the cause wasn’t an extraordinary circumstance. DGCA rules set compensation of ₹5,000 to ₹10,000 per passenger depending on flight length. Ask the airline in writing.

What if nothing works?#

File with the National Consumer Helpline (call 1915). If that doesn’t resolve it, you can file a case with the consumer commission online through e-Daakhil.

If this is happening to you#

Don’t accept a partial refund for a flight the airline cancelled without asking why. Write down the numbers, take the screenshots, and ask both the airline and the booking platform, in writing, where your money is.

Use the formal channels: AirSewa and the National Consumer Helpline exist for exactly this, and a complaint number is harder to ignore than a phone bot. And when you get your money back, share how you did it, on X, in the comments, anywhere other passengers will see it. Every resolved case makes the next one easier.
